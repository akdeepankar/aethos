Section 01

The industry at a glance

The one-line view

India is the world's #4 buyer of machine tools but only the #9 maker. Demand has tripled in five years, yet imports still meet ~59% of it by value because the 'brains and muscles' of a CNC machine - controllers, spindles, ball screws, guides - are made abroad. The Indian builders that make those parts themselves earn the best margins.

The 15 things to know

  1. A machine tool is the 'mother machine': the machine that makes every other machine. In a CNC (Computer Numerical Control) machine a computer, not a human hand, guides the cutting tool, repeatably and to within a few microns (a human hair is ~70 microns).
  2. India's machine-tool market was ~₹36,400 Cr (US$4.1bn per IMTMA) in FY26, up 15% YoY and roughly 3x FY21.
  3. Imports meet ~59% of Indian demand by value (₹21,628 Cr) but only ~40% by volume: India imports the expensive, high-end machines and makes the simpler ones.
  4. Domestic production was ~₹17,400 Cr (US$2.0bn), up 19%. That is smaller than the revenue of a single global builder like DMG Mori (~US$3.4bn) or Fanuc (~US$5.7bn).
  5. The organised core is concentrated: ~1,000 manufacturers exist, but the top 25 earn >70% of revenue and the top four (ACE Micromatic, Jyoti CNC, BFW, LMW) make ~74% of domestic CNC output value.
  6. Profit sits at the two ends of the value chain. Controller makers (Fanuc: 21% operating margin) and service/software earn the most; pure assemblers earn 4-12%.
  7. In India, integration equals margin. Jyoti CNC (in-house spindles, tool changers, rotary tables, heads) earns ~55% gross and ~25% EBITDA margins, 1,200-1,500 bps above peers that import these parts.
  8. The trade-off is cash. High-end, defence-heavy builders (Jyoti ~300-360 day cash cycle) tie up far more working capital than volume players (ACE ~53 days, LMW ~34 days).
  9. Demand is broadening beyond auto. Auto is still 29-42% of CNC demand, but defence, EMS, EVs, semiconductors and medical devices are expected to grow 15-30% a year versus ~9-12% for ICE auto.
  10. VMCs and turning centres are the volume heart (~45-50% of CNC consumption); 5-axis and turn-mill machines are small in volume but carry the highest price and strategic value.
  11. A replacement cycle is starting: ~2.95 lakh installed CNC machines, much of it bought in FY09-14, implies ~20,000 replacements a year.
  12. Global tailwinds: China tightened export screening of high-precision CNC machines in 2026, Japanese OEMs are outsourcing mid-range builds to Indian partners (DMG Mori with LMW), and Tsugami opened a ₹300 Cr plant in Chennai.
  13. The bottlenecks are Tier-2 'brains and muscles': controllers, precision spindles, ball screws, linear guides, bearings and encoders, plus casting quality and skilled assemblers.
  14. What Indian builders lack: scale, R&D depth, controller/software IP, a precision-component ecosystem, a global service footprint and a record in the most demanding applications.
  15. For investors: the sector is cyclical and working-capital heavy. Watch share gains from imports, mix upgrade (ASP), backward integration (gross margin) and cash conversion.

Section 02

What a CNC machine is, and how precision is achieved

Look at anything metal around you: a car's engine block, the aluminium body of a phone, a gear in a ceiling fan, a pump impeller, a turbine blade. Almost every one was, at some stage, shaped by a machine tool - a machine that cuts, grinds, drills or bends metal into a precise shape. Because machine tools also make the parts of every other machine, the industry is called the mother industry of manufacturing.

The most common method is subtractive: start with a block or bar of metal and remove everything that is not the part, the way a sculptor removes stone. On an old manual lathe a skilled machinist turns hand-wheels to move the tool. On a CNC machine a computer reads a program of coordinates and drives servo motors that move the tool along a path accurate to a few microns, again and again, all day and night. One operator can run several machines.

Analogy

A manual machine is a driver navigating by memory. A CNC machine is a car given exact GPS coordinates that drives itself, correcting its position hundreds of times a second. The car's iron body still has to be rigid and well built, but the navigation - controller, servos and feedback - is what makes the result repeatable.

Spindles, tool changers, rotary tables and heads alone are 20-40% of a mid-to-high-end VMC's bill of materials. A builder that makes them in-house at ~65% of the import cost gains a 7-14% cost advantage at the machine level. That, not brand, is the main reason Jyoti's gross margin is ~15-20 points above peers.

The four enemies of precision

Making a part accurate to 5 microns over half a metre is a fight against physics. A CNC machine is a mechatronic system: iron (structure) + precision mechanics (spindle, screws, guides, bearings) + electronics (drives, motors, encoders) + software (controller, CAM, compensation). India is competent at the first and increasingly at the second; the third and fourth are largely imported.

EnemyWhat happensHow the leaders beat it
Deflection (stiffness)Cutting forces push the tool and the part apartMass and rigidity: heavy Meehanite-grade cast-iron beds and columns, wide guideways, stiff spindles. Casting quality is a hidden advantage.
Heat (thermal growth)Steel grows ~11-12 microns per metre for every 1°C; motors, friction and even sunlight move the toolThermally symmetric designs, cooling and software compensation (Okuma's Thermo-Friendly Concept)
Vibration (chatter)The tool vibrates at the wrong frequency; the surface is ruined and tools breakDamping (cast iron, polymer concrete), balanced spindles, smart speed selection
Positioning errorBall screws have tiny pitch errors and backlashClosed-loop feedback: rotary encoders and linear glass scales (Heidenhain) measure the real position and the controller corrects it thousands of times a second

A short history: why the controller is the crown jewel

  1. 1940s-50sNC is born

    Numerical Control was developed in the US (John Parsons with MIT, funded by the US Air Force) to machine complex aircraft parts from punched tape.

  2. 1970sNC becomes CNC

    Cheap microprocessors turned NC into CNC. Japan's Fanuc (spun out of Fujitsu) mass-produced reliable controllers and servo motors; Japanese builders standardised on them and overtook US builders in the 1980s.

  3. 1990s-2000sThree models emerge

    Germany (Siemens, Heidenhain) held the high end; Taiwan built a mid-range ecosystem around good-enough components (HIWIN ball screws and guides); China scaled volume.

  4. 2010s-todaySoftware era

    Multi-axis machines, robot-loaded automation cells, sensors and software (digital twins, AI-assisted programming) are where differentiation now lies.

The key lesson

Whoever controls the controller and servo system controls a large share of the industry's profit and its technology direction. That is why China, despite producing ~US$30bn of machine tools, still imports most high-end controllers, and why India's ~92% dependence on four foreign controller makers matters.

The two basic families are turning (the part spins, the tool is still, so the result is round) and milling (the tool spins and moves over a clamped part to make flat faces, pockets and 3D shapes).

What 'axes' mean, and why 5-axis is the prestige segment

A 3-axis VMC can only attack the part from above; to machine the sides the part must be unclamped and re-clamped, adding time and error. A rotary table (4th axis) or a tilting/rotating head or table (5th axis) lets the tool approach from almost any angle. Simultaneous 5-axis machining is essential for turbine blades, impellers, aerostructures and implants. It is harder to build (kinematic accuracy, thermal control) and far harder to control, which is why the controllers for it are export-controlled by several countries.

The P&L drivers

  • Gross margin = mix + integration. Premium machines and in-house components lift gross margin; buying in controllers, spindles and guides caps it (Indian peers ~27-42% vs Jyoti ~52-55%).
  • Operating leverage. Employee cost, R&D and the dealer/service network are largely fixed, so EBITDA swings far more than revenue across the cycle.
  • Working capital is the real constraint. Imported components with long lead times, WIP, dealer and customer credit, and for defence, percentage-of-completion accounting that books revenue before cash. Cash cycles range from ~34 days (LMW) to ~380 (Lokesh).
  • Customer financing. Most buyers are MSME job shops that buy on bank loans, so interest rates, CGTMSE credit guarantees and equipment finance directly affect order flow.

Why the business is cyclical

Machine tools are a second-derivative business: they depend on the change in manufacturing output, not its level. When auto or electronics capacity is full, orders surge; when growth slows, orders can fall 20-40% even if production is merely flat. Globally 2023-25 was a downturn (seven of the ten largest consuming countries shrank in 2024), while India was the outlier, growing throughout.

The smile curve: who captures the profit

Profit follows scarcity. There are hundreds of machine builders worldwide, but only four firms supply ~75-80% of the world's high-end CNC controllers (Fanuc, Siemens, Heidenhain, Mitsubishi) and a handful make world-class ball screws and guides. Fanuc's FY3/26 operating margin was 21.4% on ¥857.8bn of sales, while builders like Amada (10.2%), Makino (9.6%) and DMG Mori (single-digit in 2025) earn much less.

Investor takeaway

The Indian builders that move up the smile curve - make their own components, add controllers and software, grow service - earn structurally higher margins. Jyoti (components), Macpower (own controller) and LMW (own foundry) are three versions of this strategy. Pure assemblers compete mostly on price and scale.

Estimates of the global machine-tool market range from ~US$80bn to ~US$120bn for 2025 depending on the research house (MarketsandMarkets US$81bn; GMI US$96bn; Grand View US$117bn). Two structural observations. First, the global industry is fragmented: the top four or five players hold only ~15% of the market, so there is room for a focused challenger. Second, the most successful builders own a piece of the 'brain': Okuma makes its own OSP controller and Mazak its Mazatrol programming system, which lets them differentiate on software. That is the direction Indian builders will eventually need to take.

The China lesson for India

Volume does not equal technology. China is the world's largest producer and domestic firms hold >95% of the low-end CNC market, yet foreign brands still hold >70% of the mid-range and high-end localisation is below 10%; one 2025 white paper put the domestic share of China's CNC-system market at only ~2%. Focused players can still break through: KEDE Numerical Control built its own 5-axis simultaneous controller and now supplies China's C919 aircraft programme. The Indian parallel would be Jyoti-style component integration combined with Macpower-style in-house controls, at much larger scale.

Imports are high-end by nature

Imports are ~60% of value but only ~40% of units. Domestic builders cannot yet match high-end capability, and Chinese entry-level machines undercut them on price. The ~₹21,600 Cr of imports is the prize; the ~₹8,000-9,000 Cr of mid-range imports (VMCs, HMCs, turning centres) is the most winnable near-term slice.

Jyoti CNC Automation - the integrated, high-end leader

Founded in 1989 in Rajkot as a maker of machine-tool gearboxes, Jyoti grew into India's broadest CNC builder. Its 2007 acquisition of Huron Graffenstaden (Strasbourg, France) gave it European 5-axis and high-speed milling technology, making it the only Indian builder with 5-axis capability at scale. Listed in January 2024.

How it solves the bottleneck

  • Makes spindles, tool changers, pallet changers, rotary tables and universal heads in-house
  • Import content ~22-40% of selling price vs ~38-55% for peers
  • Supplies ~75-80% of Huron's raw materials and components from India
  • Rajkot labour, land and attrition give a further cost edge
  • ~5,550 machines sold in FY26; brokers model ~12,000 by FY29E

What to watch

  • Huron export-control probe by French customs (DNRED): €3mn asset seizure, ₹67 Cr FY26 revenue reversal
  • A&D projects under percentage-of-completion keep the cash cycle very long; brokers expect ~250 days by FY29E
  • FY24-26 execution delays (Huron plant, EMS dispatches, capacity ramp)
  • Promoter pledge rose from 4.1% to 15.4% of holding in 4QFY26
  • Operating cash flow negative in FY24-25

LMW (Machine Tool Division) - the quiet, cash-efficient contract builder

LMW (formerly Lakshmi Machine Works, Coimbatore) is best known as India's leading textile-machinery maker, but its Machine Tool Division, together with its foundry, has become the group's growth and profit engine. It makes VMCs, HMCs, turning centres and turn-mill machines, and runs one of the better captive foundries in the industry.

Bottlenecks, who solves them, and what India lacks

BottleneckWhy it mattersWho in India solves it bestHow
1. CNC controllers & servo systemsThe brain: ~25%+ of machine cost, sets capability (5-axis), carries IP and export-control riskMacpower (Macatrol); CMTI and academic programmesIn-house controller for part of the range; still sub-scale nationally (~92% imported)
2. Spindles, ATCs, rotary tables, heads20-40% of BOM of mid/high VMCs; set speed, accuracy, uptimeJyoti CNCFull in-house manufacture at ~65% of import cost → 7-14% machine-level cost edge

4. Casting qualityStiffness, damping and long-term accuracyLMW (captive foundry); Tsugami (new Chennai foundry); Rajkot baseCaptive or cluster foundries with stress-relieving and QC
5. Skilled assembly & application engineeringHand-scraping, alignment and process design separate good from great machinesACE, Jyoti, BFW via in-house training; IMTMAAcademies, technology centres, customer application labs
6. Capacity & job-work dependenceGrowth limited by machining capacity for own componentsJyoti (16,000/yr); Macpower (new site); ACE (Gujarat plant)Capacity additions, less outsourced job-work

What Indian companies are doing about it

  • PositiveCapacity: Jyoti 6,000 → 16,000 machines; ACE's Gujarat plant; Macpower's new 13-acre site.
  • PositivePremiumisation: Macpower's NEXA and double-column machines; Jyoti's 5-axis and multi-tasking lines; everyone targeting defence and aerospace.
  • PositiveBackward integration: Jyoti (components), LMW (foundry), Macpower (controller).
  • PositiveRiding global OEMs: LMW building for DMG Mori; Japanese firms localising in India.
  • PositiveExports +76% in FY26; Jyoti says its cost advantage is opening export markets, even China.
  • WatchDigital and Industry 4.0: connected machines and software (Jyoti's '7th Sense') as buyers prefer new smart machines over retrofits.
  • WatchPrivate capital: large PE investment in ACE shows institutional money now sees the sector as investable.

What Indian companies still lack

  1. Scale. All of India's production (~US$2bn) is less than one DMG Mori. Scale funds R&D, global service and component purchasing power.
  2. Controller and software IP. Without their own brain, Indian builders pay the controller maker's margin and cannot differentiate on software, automation or digital services.
  3. A precision-component ecosystem. India has no Taiwan-style supplier cluster for ball screws, guides, bearings and encoders.
  4. R&D depth. Indian builders typically spend ~1-2% of revenue on R&D; global leaders spend meaningfully more, with decades of know-how.
  5. Track record at the top end. Aero-engine and 5-axis simultaneous work needs years of proven performance and customer approvals.
  6. Global service footprint and brand. Only Jyoti (via Huron) has a meaningful overseas footprint.
  7. Aftermarket monetisation. Service, spares and software are a small share of revenue, versus a large, stable profit pool abroad.

Key risks

  1. Cyclicality

    A slowdown in private capex (tariffs, rates, global demand) would hit orders quickly, and operating leverage works both ways.

  2. Currency

    A weak yen makes Japanese machines cheaper in India; it can also make imported components cheaper for Indian builders.

  3. Chinese competition

    Entry-level machines are not covered by China's 2026 controls; excess Chinese capacity could keep pressure on entry-level pricing.

  4. Working capital and execution

    Many Indian builders have long cash cycles; rapid growth can burn cash.

  5. Technology and export controls

    Dual-use technology rules (as in Jyoti's Huron probe) can affect high-end players.

  6. Governance

    Promoter pledges, related-party dealings and percentage-of-completion accounting need scrutiny.

Questions to ask management

  1. What share of your bill of materials is imported today, and what will it be in three years? Which components are next to be localised?
  2. What share of your machines use your own controller (or could), and what stops you from doing more?
  3. How is average realisation per machine moving, and what drives it: mix, features or price?
  4. What is your aftermarket (service, spares, retrofits, software) revenue share, and what is the target?
  5. Where do you lose orders to imports - which machine types, which customers, and why?
  6. How much of your order book is defence/aerospace, and what is its cash-flow profile?
  7. How have China's export controls and Japanese outsourcing changed your competitive position?
  8. What are your machine uptime/MTBF metrics versus Japanese competitors, and how do you prove them?
  9. What is the cash-cycle target and how will new capacity change it?
